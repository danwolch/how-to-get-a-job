## Networking is how you actually apply

Every open role online gets flooded with applicants, and more of them now come from people using AI to apply to hundreds of jobs a week. A resume that arrives cold competes with all of them. A resume that arrives with someone saying "you should talk to this person" skips the line.

Ethan Evans spent years hiring at Amazon. He puts it from the hiring manager's side: when a big role is open, there are people they know and people they don't, and a bad hire is painful enough that they default to the ones they know. Someone vouching for you is how you move from the second group to the first.

<Clip id="evans-people-i-know" />

Recruiters work the same way. Lauren Ipsen, who has filled senior product roles for venture-backed companies, doesn't start a search by messaging strangers on LinkedIn. She starts by asking a handful of people she trusts.

<Clip id="ipsen-favorite-leaders" />

So networking isn't a side activity you do while applying. It's how you apply. Before each conversation, tailor your resume for the person and company you're about to talk to (that's covered in [step five](/applying/#tailor-the-application)).

The playbook changes a bit depending on [which path you're on](/story/#which-of-three-paths-are-you-on), but the mechanics below apply to everyone.

## Why you avoid it, and why you shouldn't

The fear is the ask. Asking feels like imposing, or like admitting you need help.

But the people who know you probably like you, and most of them want to help. The worst realistic outcome is a no, and a no is expected and fine. People are busy for all sorts of private reasons, and they may never tell you why. It's about their life, not you.

Ada Chen Rekhi, an executive coach, names the misconception directly: people assume asking for advice is one-sided. It isn't. Helping someone feels good, especially when it only takes a few minutes and you later hear how it turned out.

<Clip id="chen-rekhi-feels-good" />

## Why helping you is in their interest

Being known as someone who connects good people is a good reputation to have. When someone meets a person who's clearly good, they're usually keen to introduce them, because it reflects well on them.

Don't let that turn into pressure. Moving someone from "sure, I guess" to "I have to introduce you to my friend" isn't hard:

- Show up on time.
- Smile, and be appreciative and enthusiastic.
- Tell the best truthful version of your story.
- Make clear what you can offer.

A little effort here gets people making introductions for you without being asked twice.

## Who to talk to first

Order your list by how well you know people.

**Strong ties first.** Close friends, former teammates, people who've seen your work. You can be honest with them about your uncertainty, they'll cheer you on, and early wins build confidence. But they share most of your network, so they're rarely your best leads.

**Then weak ties.** Acquaintances, former colleagues you haven't spoken to in years, friends of friends. These are usually the most valuable conversations, because they know people you don't. The sociologist Mark Granovetter called this "the strength of weak ties" in 1973, and his finding that people often hear about jobs through acquaintances rather than close friends has held up since. Practice on warm ties, then go lukewarm, then weak.

**Then cold, through affinity.** Your network is bigger than you think. Your university and grad school alumni, everyone who has worked at any of your former companies, your running group, your gym, the parents at your kid's school. Anything alum-shaped gives you a reason to reach out.

## Match the ask to the relationship

The weaker the tie and the less sure you are of your direction, the more you should ask for advice rather than a role.

Asking a stranger to refer you for a job you're not qualified for makes you look clueless. Asking the same stranger "you made the move from consulting to product; how did you decide?" makes you look thoughtful, and often ends with them offering to help anyway.

This is hard to judge about yourself, which is why the [story work](/story/) matters. The best forcing function is reading the reactions on your early calls.

When you're unsure, Phyl Terry's "golden question" is the best advice ask I know. Terry wrote *Never Search Alone* and has run job search councils for thousands of people. He sends job seekers on a "listening tour" of people they trust, and gives them one question to ask: *If you were in my shoes, how would you approach this?* It asks for their judgment without asking them to decide on the spot whether to vouch for you.

## Read the room, then fix the story

Networking is a learning loop. Every conversation tells you whether your story is landing.

| Good signs | Warning signs |
| --- | --- |
| They nod, say "that makes sense" and move on | They look skeptical or ask lots of clarifying questions |
| They volunteer a name or offer an intro unprompted | They say "let me think about who might know" and nothing follows |
| They repeat your story back accurately | They describe you as something you're not aiming for |
| They tell you a company that fits | They suggest something completely different |

If you keep getting warning signs, the problem is usually the story, not the delivery. Go back and narrow it.

## Record your calls

Ask if you can record "for my own self-improvement." It signals a growth mindset, and framed that way, almost nobody says no.

Afterwards, review the call, or have an impartial AI do it. What did they ask? Where did they seem confused? How did you deliver your main points? Did you actually make the ask? You'll learn more from one listen-back than from ten calls you only remember. (Work Coach does this review automatically if you want it done for you.)

## Two goals for every conversation

**1. Get them to like you.** People help people they like. Be enthusiastic, on time, confident and lively, someone they'd want to be associated with. If you're on path one, project confidence fully. Never lie or fake it: put the best truthful version of yourself forward. This gets easier with reps.

**2. Make it easy to help.** Go in with a specific ask and a forwardable blurb ready to send.

Marc Baselga, co-host of Supra Insider, describes getting exactly this kind of note from a friend who'd just been laid off. Instead of "connect me to all these people," it explained what happened, what he was looking for and what he was doing in the meantime. The ask was light, and that made him want to help.

<Clip id="baselga-light-ask" />

## End every conversation with an ask

Most people do this badly, or skip it. Every call ends with an ask, whatever path you're on. If you're seeking advice, the advice is part of the ask, but there's usually a person, company, role or resource to ask for on top. You can't stay in advice mode forever.

- **If they don't offer, ask directly.** "Would you be open to making a couple of introductions for me?"
- **The more specific, the better.** Name people or companies you've already found, for example by browsing their LinkedIn connections before the call. Your homework is what makes it easy for them.
- **A specific ask is a prompt, not a ceiling.** People forget who's in their network. Naming one person jogs their memory and they'll surface others. Lead with a name, then ask: "Who else comes to mind?"

<Example label="The ask, word for word">

"This has been really helpful, thank you. I noticed you're connected to Priya Shah at Northwind. Would you be open to introducing me? I'd love to hear how her team thinks about growth. And is there anyone else who comes to mind I should talk to?"

</Example>

**Compounding is the whole point.** If every call yields two introductions, you go from "I have no network" to having too many people to talk to, fast. Almost nobody thinks about networking this way. The risk isn't running out of people. It's not asking for enough, and not being proactive enough afterwards.

How many conversations is enough? Andrew Yeung, who hosts tech networking events in New York, gives a simple target for anyone actively searching: at least five new conversations a week.

<Clip id="yeung-five-conversations" />

## How introductions should work

The standard is a **double opt-in**. Your contact first asks the other person whether they're willing to talk, and only connects you once they say yes. That way, everyone who gets on a call with you actually wants to be there.

If your contact doesn't say that's how they'll do it, check: "Would you mind asking them first if they're up for it? I'll send you a short blurb you can forward."

Phyl Terry is blunt about why. Anyone sent to him through an intro he didn't agree to is, in his words, someone he doesn't want to speak to. His fix is the same as ours: ask your contact to get permission first, and write the request for them in their voice so it costs them nothing.

<Clip id="terry-permission-intros" />

## Write the forwardable blurb

This is the key to a good intro. Send your contact an email they can read, think "great," and forward in one click. Frame it as "here's a blurb you can easily forward." You don't need to add "feel free to edit."

What it has to do:

- **Say why the recipient should take the call.** You're selling.
- **Sound like the introducer**, so it's not too salesy.
- **Show the introducer understands** what's interesting from the recipient's side and what you have to offer.
- **Make the introducer look good** for connecting high-quality people.

<Example label="A forwardable blurb">

Subject: Intro to Maya Chen?

Hi Priya, would you be up for a quick intro to Maya Chen? She ran growth for the consumer app at Ledgerly, where she took week-one retention from 31% to 44% by rebuilding onboarding, and she's now looking at senior growth roles in fintech. Given what you mentioned about activation being Northwind's big focus this year, I think you'd get a lot out of 20 minutes with her. Happy to connect you if you're up for it.

</Example>

<Prompt title="Write a forwardable intro blurb">
```text
Write a short email I can send to my contact so they can forward it to someone they're introducing me to.

Me: [YOUR TWO-SENTENCE STORY, plus 1–2 specific results]
My contact (the introducer): [NAME, how they know me]
The person I want to meet: [NAME, ROLE, COMPANY, anything I know about their priorities]
What I want from the conversation: [e.g. 20 minutes on how their team approaches X]

Rules:
- Write it in the introducer's voice, in third person about me, so they can forward it unchanged.
- Under 100 words. One concrete result, one reason this person in particular would find the conversation worthwhile.
- Warm but not salesy. No adjectives like "amazing" or "rockstar".
- End by offering to connect us if they're up for it (double opt-in).

Give me two versions: one more formal, one more casual.
```
</Prompt>

## Follow up the same day

Most people skip follow-up, which is why doing it well stands out.

- **Send a thank-you the same day**, including the forwardable blurb for any intros they offered. Send it even if they couldn't offer anything.
- **After you meet the person they introduced you to, close the loop with the introducer** in one short sentence: "Talked to Priya today, great conversation, she's introducing me to her VP." Make them glad they did it.

Sam Lessin, a partner at Slow Ventures, says the thank-you should be short and genuine: we met, I got something out of it, thank you for your time.

## Cold outreach

With no introducer, the message has to earn the reply on its own. Lead with the shared affinity and ask for advice, never for a role or an introduction.

- **Channel:** email first, ideally from an alumni directory or community. If you can't find an email, use a LinkedIn connection request with a note.
- **Craft it, don't mass-send it.** It's obvious when someone put in effort. But don't over-research either. Scan their LinkedIn, find one thing that shows you did your homework ("saw you moved from consulting into product"), then make the ask.
- **About five sentences:** the affinity, the one homework detail, who you are, the advice ask, a low-pressure close.
- **Volume:** more than you'd send to friends, but you can't and shouldn't email a thousand people. Start small and ramp up.
- **Expect a low hit rate, and don't judge yourself by it.** Most people either skip cold outreach or do it badly and quit. Very few people in any alumni group ever get asked for advice. The successful-but-not-famous alum is almost never approached, and is often delighted to be. Measure the enthusiasm of the people who reply, not the silence of the ones who don't.
- **Follow up once** if you hear nothing, about a week later.

One caveat for very busy or senior people: some of them will answer a sharp written question but won't take a 15-minute call with a stranger. Jules Walter, a product leader at YouTube, found some of his mentors through cold messages that asked one very clear question. Lenny Rachitsky adds in the same conversation that he has no time for coffee chat requests but will answer a real question quickly. If your target is famous, ask the question in writing and let the call be their idea.

<Clip id="walter-specific-context" />

<Example label="A cold email">

Hi Sarah,

I'm a fellow Michigan alum and saw you moved from consulting into product at Stripe a few years ago. I'm a product manager who was just laid off from a fintech startup, and I'm trying to decide between going big-company or staying early-stage. Your path is exactly the one I'm weighing, and I'd really value fifteen minutes of your perspective on how you made that call. Happy to work around your schedule, and thanks either way.

Dan

</Example>
