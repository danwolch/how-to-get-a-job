## The power shifts

Until the official offer, you're one of many candidates moving through a funnel. The moment they make an offer, that flips. They've named the person they want, and now they want to close you.

Why it flips:

- **The hiring manager needs someone in the seat.** Every week without one hurts their team.
- **The recruiter is measured on funnel metrics:** applications, screens, interviews, days to close, and above all, how many offers get accepted. Once the company has picked you, a no from you is a miss for them.
- **The recruiter works for the hiring manager, not for you.** Be friendly, but remember whose interests they represent.

The flip side of that metric is that recruiters push for a fast yes. You can almost always ask for more time. Aakash Gupta, a former VP of Product at Apollo, estimates hiring managers grant it around 80% of the time. Use the time well, for example to get informal references on the company and your future manager from people who've worked there.

<Clip id="gupta-more-time" />

None of this gives you license to do whatever you want. It gives you the confidence to find out which parts of the offer can move, and to ask how they can make it a yes.

## Know your market

In an ideal world you'd have several offers in flight, so they play off each other. You won't always be there, and how much leverage you have depends on the market. In 2026, most white-collar hiring is a buyer's market: employers have the edge. Know which one you're in.

Either way, know the market range for the role and level. Use salary data sites, posted ranges for similar roles (many US states now require them), and people in your network at similar companies. There's almost always room to negotiate, and not negotiating is itself a signal, especially at senior levels.

Ethan Evans, who set pay for a lot of people as an Amazon VP, says recruiters almost always leave room in an offer, because they expect you to ask. Taking the first number, in his words, makes you "pretty much a sucker."

## The variables

An offer has more parts than salary:

- Base salary
- Bonus, commission or other incentive pay
- Equity, and its vesting schedule
- Signing bonus
- Start date
- Title and level
- Sometimes: vacation, remote flexibility, relocation, a learning budget

Companies differ in which of these they can move. Base salary is often tied to a band. Signing bonuses and equity are often more flexible. Evans has traded salary for an extra week of vacation.

### Salary and title go together

Titles come with pay bands. Know where you'd sit in the band. A product manager at the top of the band who gets promoted to senior often sees little or no raise, because they were already paid like one.

Sometimes a higher title at a slightly lower salary is the better deal. If you perform, the pay gets fixed, and the title compounds into your next role. You're optimizing for two things at once: the best offer now, and where you'll be six to twelve months in if it goes well.

## Do it on the phone

Negotiate live, on the phone or a video call, not over email. You learn far more from someone's voice than from their writing, and they'll usually want a call anyway. Use email only to confirm what you agreed.

They'll put pressure on you. "I need to think it over" is always available, and nobody serious will hold it against you.

**Call one: the offer comes in.**

- Thank them, and be genuinely excited.
- Ask how flexible the parts are: base, bonus, equity, signing, level, start date. "Which of these have the most room?"
- Ask, innocently: "Is this as high as you can go?"
- Say you need a little time to think it over, and agree on when you'll get back to them.

**Call two: your one ask.** After you've seen the whole offer and thought about it, make a single request that covers everything: "If you can get to 130 with a 20 signing bonus, I'll sign today." More on this below.

<Prompt title="Plan my offer negotiation call">
```text
Help me prepare for a salary negotiation phone call.

The offer: [base, bonus, equity and vesting, signing bonus, title/level, start date, anything else]
The role and location: [ROLE, LEVEL, CITY or REMOTE]
What I know about the market range: [DATA, SOURCES, POSTED RANGES]
Other offers or processes in flight (be honest; I won't bluff): [DETAILS or "none"]
What matters most to me, in order: [e.g. base, title, start date]
My walk-away point: [OPTIONAL]

1. Tell me whether this offer looks low, fair or strong for the market, and what you're basing that on. Flag what you're unsure about.
2. Draft talking points for call one: thanking them, asking how flexible each part is, asking "is this as high as you can go?", and asking for time.
3. Draft my single final ask for call two, in the form "If you can do X, I'll sign," covering all the variables at once. Give me a primary version and a fallback that trades across variables.
4. Write responses for: "this is our best offer", "we need an answer by Friday", and "what number would make this work?"

Tone: warm, enthusiastic about the role, firm on substance. No bluffing about competing offers.
```
</Prompt>

## Who to negotiate with

My advice: keep the money conversations with the recruiter, calm and friendly, and save your future manager's goodwill. If things get stuck, your manager can step in and save the day, and that's a good way to start a working relationship. (At a startup without a recruiter, the founder plays both roles. Same calm tone, more care.)

Phyl Terry, author of *Never Search Alone*, strongly disagrees. He wants you negotiating live with the hiring manager whenever the company allows it.

<Clip id="terry-hiring-manager" />

His version starts with what you need to succeed in the role, such as budget, team and support, before you talk about money, so the hiring manager goes to bat for you internally. That's good advice, and it's compatible with mine. Talk to the hiring manager about scope and what success needs. Keep the back-and-forth over numbers with the recruiter, so your first real negotiation with your future boss isn't about your own pay.

## Don't move the goalposts

The most annoying thing a candidate can do is ask for things one at a time: first base, then once that's settled, equity, then the start date. Every new ask reopens the deal and burns goodwill.

Ask early how flexible each variable is, see the whole offer, then state your preferences across all of them at once.

## Push hard on substance, stay warm in tone

The company wants you. In an ideal negotiation you ask for something and they say no. That's healthy: it means you didn't leave anything on the table. It's fine to keep going until "this is the best we can do."

Salary compounds over a career, and changing jobs is the moment to move it. Most people are nervous and don't push hard enough. Phyl Terry cites a long-running figure that people who ask for more money get it the large majority of the time. It's lower in a tough market, but you can still ask.

<Clip id="terry-87-percent" />

Being likable and negotiating hard don't conflict. There's a big difference between "take it or leave it" and "do you have any room to improve this?"

It's also very rare for a company to pull an offer because someone asked what's possible. Josh Doody, who has coached many people through negotiations, has never seen a big or medium-sized tech company do it. By the time they make an offer, they've spent a lot of money and time on interviews.

<Clip id="doody-never-pulled" />

## Make one final ask

After a couple of rounds, once you sense where their ceiling is, make one final request. They went from 100 to 120; you say, "130 gets it done." They can say no. If you frame it with real interest in the role, as working with them rather than squeezing them, it won't get the offer pulled.

Doody does the same thing, and he makes it explicit that it's the last request: one thing, and if they can say yes, you're done.

<Clip id="doody-one-thing" />

**If you say a number gets it done and they hit it, you sign.** Don't keep asking. That's the fastest way to turn a great start into a bad one.

## Competing offers and deadlines

- **Never lie about having other offers.** It's easy to check, and it's a terrible way to start.
- **If you have a real deadline on an offer elsewhere**, tell your top-choice companies, even before they've made an offer, so they can speed up. You're looking out for yourself and avoiding being forced to accept for lack of alternatives. Only do this when you're late in their process and have a good relationship with the recruiter.
- **Only one offer, nothing else cooking?** Negotiate exactly the same way. Market range, warm tone, keep going until they say no.

## Play the long game

You never know what happens next. Offers get rescinded for reasons outside anyone's control, like a hiring freeze or layoffs. Or you accept somewhere else, hate it, and come back six months later to a company that says "great, let's do it."

So advocate for yourself warmly and reasonably, and leave every company thinking: reasonable, good to deal with, we'd love to work with them if things change.

## Accepting

Be enthusiastic. Settle the start date and any other details before you sign. The last thing you want is "Yes, I accept!" followed by "Oh, one more thing..." Set yourself up to start well.

## Declining

However you feel about them, thank them, say you're sorry it didn't work out, and that you hope to work together in the future. You never know. Do it the same day you decide, because they're probably holding a backup candidate. Leave everyone thinking: gracious, worth the time we invested, I'd work with them anytime.

<Example label="Declining an offer">

"Thank you so much for the offer, and for the time you and the team put into the process. After a lot of thought, I've decided to accept another role that's a closer fit for what I'm focused on right now. It was a hard call. I really enjoyed getting to know the team, and I hope our paths cross again."

</Example>

## Close the loop with everyone who helped

Once you've signed, tell everyone who made an introduction, took a call or referred you. Your tracker has the list. Keep it short and sweet, with real appreciation. These are the people who got you here, and the ones you'll call next time. Some day soon, someone will ask you for an introduction. Say yes.

<Example label="Closing the loop">

"Quick update and a big thank you: I've accepted a senior PM role at Northwind, starting November 3. Your intro to Priya was the conversation that got things moving. I really appreciate you taking the time. If I can ever return the favor, please ask."

</Example>

<Checklist>

- You know the market range and which parts of the offer can move.
- You negotiated on the phone, warmly, and made one final ask across all variables.
- If they met your number, you signed.
- You declined other offers the same day, graciously.
- Everyone who helped you heard from you within a week.

</Checklist>
